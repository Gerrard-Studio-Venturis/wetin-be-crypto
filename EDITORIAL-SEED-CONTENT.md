## A-EX-01 — Why a stablecoin can still surprise you

Format: Evergreen explainer. Identity: existing A-EX-01, proposed seed revision 0.1. Prepared: 7 October 2026. Site publication: unset.

### Reader copy

A stablecoin aims to track a reference value, often the US dollar. That aim can be useful, but it does not answer every question about buying, holding, sending or converting the token. A familiar label still needs a few checks.

**What supports the target?** Stablecoins use different designs. Some depend on issuer-held reserves; others use crypto collateral or different mechanisms. Find out which model applies, who is responsible, and what could stop the system working as intended. Calling every model “backed by dollars” hides those differences.

**What price are you being offered?** A token targeting USD 1 can trade above or below that value. A quote in naira, cedis or another currency also depends on the offered exchange rate, spread and charges. A larger displayed balance does not tell you the final amount available through a conversion route.

**Can you redeem directly with the issuer?** Owning a token and being eligible for direct issuer redemption are separate things. Circle's inspected non-EEA USDC terms require an eligible Circle Mint account and apply conditions to direct redemption. Someone offering to buy your tokens is a different route with different responsibilities. Other issuers and jurisdictions need their own checked terms.

**Who controls access?** A provider-held balance depends on that provider's account and withdrawal rules. With self-custody, your recovery arrangements matter. Neither model removes issuer restrictions or every other dependency. Under the Circle terms inspected for this draft, USDC holdings in Circle Mint are not described as deposit-insured bank savings. A separate lending product adds separate conditions; holding USDC itself does not establish an interest entitlement under those terms.

**Will the destination accept the exact token?** Check the representation, selected network and current receiving instructions together. A matching ticker or similar-looking address is not enough. A third-party wrapped version must not be assumed interchangeable with an issuer-native deployment.

Small print fit change the whole story.

The useful result is knowing which claim has been checked and which remains uncertain. You may choose to pause, ask for clearer information, or use a familiar payment method that better fits the situation. Understanding the conditions comes before deciding what to do.

## A-EX-02 — Who controls a wallet, and what can be recovered?

Format: Evergreen explainer. Prepared: 7 October 2026. Site publication: unset.

### Reader copy

Two people can both say “I have a wallet” while describing different arrangements. One uses a service account whose provider controls the transfer keys. Another uses software that lets them authorise transactions themselves. Before choosing either, ask who can move the assets, what you must protect, and what happens if access is lost.

**A service account:** You may sign in with a password and another authentication step, while the provider manages the underlying keys. Account recovery depends on that service's actual procedures and eligibility checks. Recovering a password does not guarantee that withdrawals will be available: account restrictions, provider operations and receiving conditions can still matter.

**Self-custody:** You control a required part of transaction authority. In a common design, a private key authorises activity and a recovery phrase can restore related keys in compatible software. Losing every valid recovery route can mean losing access. Someone obtaining a usable key or phrase may gain dangerous control. Keep those secrets out of messages, support forms and this learning website.

There are also shared-control, hardware-assisted and other wallet designs. Some use multiple approvals; others provide different recovery arrangements. A recovery phrase is not a universal feature of every wallet. The useful question is about the specific model rather than a slogan about ownership.

Consider a fictional comparison. Service A offers password recovery but publishes its own withdrawal conditions. Wallet B lets its user authorise transfers directly and provides a documented recovery process. That does not make A automatically safe or B automatically suitable. The responsibilities differ. Read current instructions and consider whether you can maintain the required recovery arrangement.

An address may be shared for receiving where appropriate. A private key or recovery phrase is a different kind of information. A person asking for those secrets is not merely asking where to send money. A signature request also needs inspection: some authorise consequential actions, rather than simply confirming a sign-in.

Before you rely on a wallet, be able to explain its control, recovery and support arrangements in ordinary words. Check instructions through an independently reached official source. If important details remain unclear, pause. This article asks you to understand the model; it does not ask you to create a wallet, disclose a secret or transfer funds.

**Explore further:** [Wallets and custody](#h-wallets--wallets-and-custody), [Scams and trust](#h-scams--scams-and-trust), and M-03 when published.

## A-EX-03 — What proves a crypto payment reached its destination?

Format: Evergreen explainer. Prepared: 7 October 2026. Site publication: unset.

### Reader copy

A message saying “sent” can describe the start of a process. It does not establish every later result. A screenshot, a network transaction, a service's credited balance and a local-money payout answer different questions.

**What does the sender claim?** A screenshot can show an amount, an address and a convincing tick. It can also be edited, incomplete or taken from another transaction. Treat it as a claim to verify. A transaction identifier gives you something to investigate; it is not proof by itself.

**What does the correct network record show?** Establish the network first, then inspect a trustworthy route to that record. Check the actual status, destination, relevant asset or token movement and amount. Pending means the outcome is still unresolved. Included does not always mean the intended action succeeded: a failed smart-contract execution can still appear on a network and may incur a fee. Interpret the relevant record rather than a colour alone.

**Has the receiving service credited it?** A service may require the supported asset/network combination, destination details, a minimum deposit and a stated confirmation or processing condition. Those are specific instructions, not one universal rule. A successful transfer to a service-controlled address does not independently prove that your account has been credited.

**Has the promised payout arrived?** A credited crypto balance is different from a completed conversion and from money arriving in a bank or mobile-money account. Check the relevant provider and destination records for each step. Network finality cannot confirm a separate bank transfer.

Here is a fictional example. A receiving service says it requires five confirmations followed by processing. The supplied network record shows five confirmations and the service independently shows **Credited**. That supports the crypto-credit result under these supplied conditions. If a later local payout still says Pending, local-money receipt remains unconfirmed. We can recognise the established result without pretending the whole chain is finished.

Do not rely on an apparent payment to release goods until the evidence meets the relevant receiving conditions. If something does not match, use independently reached official support and keep the uncertainty explicit. A stranger's link or “release fee” demand does not become trustworthy because it arrived with a screenshot.

**Explore further:** M-02, M-07, [Scams and trust](#h-scams--scams-and-trust) and the transaction/confirmation/finality glossary entries when published. All figures and service conditions in the example are invented for learning.

## A-EX-04 — Same coin name, different route

Format: Evergreen explainer. Prepared: 7 October 2026. Site publication: unset.

### Reader copy

Imagine the sender's screen says USDC and the receiver's screen also says USDC. It looks like a match. Before sending, there is another question: are both sides describing the same supported asset on the same network?

A ticker is a short label. It is not a complete asset identity. A token can have deployments on different networks, and another representation can use a familiar label. A provider decides which exact combinations it accepts through a particular receiving route. Matching names cannot answer that question for you.

Check three things together.

**The asset or representation:** Is this the issuer-native deployment the receiver accepts, or something wrapped or otherwise different? Do not infer equivalence from a logo, ticker or displayed price. Use the exact identifying information supplied in current official instructions.

**The selected network:** Which network will carry the transfer? A familiar address format may appear across networks. An address that passes a format check does not establish that the receiving service credits transfers through the selected network.

**The receiving conditions:** Does the receiver explicitly support this asset on that network through this destination? Check required memo/tag details, minimums and relevant deposit conditions. “We support USDC” can be less specific than the instructions you need.

For a fictional practice example, Service A offers issuer-native USDC on Ethereum. Service B's current supplied instructions accept issuer-native USDC through Base only. The common token name does not establish a compatible route. Pause and find an explicitly supported combination. This invented setup does not claim that any real provider currently has those exact services.

Even a compatible combination is only one part of transfer review. The intended recipient, complete destination, amount, fees and meaningful conditions still need checking. You may also decide to use another payment method. A low fee cannot repair a route mismatch.

Address look familiar? Still check the route.

If something has already been sent incorrectly, recovery depends on the exact asset, network, receiving authority and provider policy. Some cases may be unrecoverable. Do not promise recovery or make a second payment to someone claiming they can fix it. Reach the genuine receiving service through an independently verified channel.

## A-STORY-01 — The screenshot that needed one more question

Format: Original fictional story/case. Prepared: 7 October 2026. Site publication: unset.

### Reader copy

**This story is invented for learning.** The people, service names, transaction records and conditions are fictional. It describes no reported incident, actual provider or country-specific payment instruction.

Nia sells a second-hand desk. A prospective buyer, Kojo, asks whether she can receive a crypto payment through a service called Harbour. Their fictional agreement requires the corresponding local-money payout to be independently recorded before she releases the desk. She has read the receiving instructions in the supplied practice pack; a credited crypto balance alone does not meet their agreement.

Kojo sends a screenshot. It shows “Transfer submitted,” 30 DEMO tokens and a transaction identifier. “All done,” his message says. He asks her to hand the desk to his driver.

Nia nearly treats the tick as a receipt. Then she asks a more precise question: what does “submitted” establish?

The practice record on the correct fictional network says **Pending**. Harbour's own practice record shows no credit. The screenshot has not established receipt. Nia says she will wait for the stated conditions rather than guess from the sender's screen.

For this exercise, Harbour requires the specified asset and destination on the correct network, five confirmations and its own processing step. These are invented conditions, not a rule for every service. Nia checks that the asset, network, destination and amount match the supplied instructions.

Later, an updated network record shows a successful transfer with five confirmations. Harbour independently shows **Credited: 30 DEMO**. Nia can now recognise that the crypto balance has been credited under the exercise's conditions. The first screenshot alone could not establish that result.

There is a final distinction. She also requested a fictional conversion and payout. Its record says **Payout pending**. Her bank practice record has no corresponding incoming payment. Credited crypto has not proved that local money has arrived. She recognises the credited crypto balance, but keeps the desk until the independently checked local-money receiving record meets their stated agreement.

Nia's useful move was to ask which result the evidence supported at each stage. She did not assume every record was false, and she did not treat one established step as proof of the next. Depending on the agreement, unresolved evidence can mean waiting, requesting clarification or choosing another route.

For your own reading of a payment claim, ask: **What is claimed? What record independently supports it? What still remains unconfirmed?** This fictional case is a thinking exercise, not an instruction to accept crypto or release goods under a universal rule.

## H-BTC — Bitcoin

### Public introduction

Bitcoin can refer to a network and to its native asset, commonly labelled BTC. The network records transfers according to its rules. A wallet helps a person interact with those records and authorise activity; the assets are not simply files sitting inside the wallet app.

Start by separating the asset, the network and any provider holding a balance for you. A service's BTC account, a self-custody arrangement and a token representing bitcoin through another system have different conditions. A familiar name does not make every receiving route interchangeable.

Bitcoin's price can move substantially. A transfer's network status does not establish a local payout, and the ability to receive bitcoin does not settle a country's legal or provider conditions. Learn the mechanics before deciding whether a particular use suits you.

## H-ETH — Ethereum

### Public introduction

Ethereum is a network that supports transfers and smart-contract execution. Ether, commonly labelled ETH, is its native asset. Other tokens can operate through Ethereum without becoming the same asset as ETH.

Those distinctions matter when reviewing a request. A transfer, contract interaction and token-spending approval may give different authority or have different results. Know what the action authorises, which network it uses and which asset or representation is involved. A successful network record does not automatically establish provider credit or a later payout.

Related networks and services can use familiar address formats and labels. Receiving support must still be checked against exact current instructions. Fee estimates also depend on the action and network conditions; a low advertised fee is not a complete transfer review.

## H-SCAMS — Scams and trust

### Public introduction

A believable message can still ask you to take an unsafe or unverified action. Urgency, familiar branding, a friend forwarding a link or a payment screenshot cannot replace evidence about the request itself.

Practise identifying what is being claimed and what authority the requested action gives away. Requests for signing secrets deserve particular care. A signature or spending permission may have consequences beyond the label shown in a message. Verify through an independently reached official source rather than the sender's preferred link.

You do not have to prove someone committed fraud before declining an unverified request. Pause when evidence is missing and avoid sending more money or secrets to a stranger claiming they can repair a loss. If a real incident occurs, follow appropriate genuine reporting/support routes; this topic is education rather than individual recovery assistance.

Stablecoins aim to track a reference value, often a currency. They use different mechanisms and can have different issuers, collateral, restrictions and redemption arrangements. The shared category name does not make them interchangeable.

## Glossary — 24 initial concepts

Each definition is draft reader copy. Examples explain the concept rather than instruct a real transaction. The 24 identities below correspond exactly to the adopted manifest; aliases do not increase the count. G-NETWORK retains its existing identity. The remaining IDs are proposed canonical glossary keys for editorial handoff, to be reconciled with the eventual CMS identity schema. Source review must address each relevant claim; listing a related hub is navigation rather than external evidence.

### G-CRYPTO-ASSET — Crypto asset

A digital asset whose ownership or use is recorded through a crypto system. The term includes assets with different designs and purposes; it does not mean every asset is money, a company share or legally classified the same way. Identify the particular asset and relevant rules before interpreting a claim about it. Related: Bitcoin, Ethereum, stablecoins.

### G-NETWORK — Blockchain network

A system of participants and rules that maintains a blockchain record and processes activity. Different networks have different assets, fees and supported actions. A familiar address format does not prove that two networks share receiving support. For a transfer, the selected network must match the destination's applicable instructions. Alias for discovery: network. Related: token representation, receiving address.

### G-WALLET — Wallet

Software or another interface used to interact with a crypto system and manage access or authorise activity. Assets are represented by system records rather than stored as ordinary coins inside the app. Wallet designs differ in who controls keys and how recovery works. Read the particular arrangement rather than assuming every wallet is self-custody. Related: custody, private key.

### G-CUSTODY — Custody

The arrangement for controlling access to assets and authorising their movement. A provider may hold keys, a person may control them directly, or authority may be shared. Custody affects responsibilities and recovery, but the label alone does not establish safety or availability. Ask who can act, what approvals are required and what restrictions apply. Related: wallet, recovery phrase.

### G-RECEIVING-ADDRESS — Receiving address

An identifier used for a destination within a crypto system. Check the complete destination and selected network through current receiving instructions. Some services also require a memo or tag. A valid-looking address does not establish the intended recipient, supported asset or provider credit. An address may be shared for receiving where appropriate; signing secrets are different. Related: memo/tag, private key.

### G-PRIVATE-KEY — Private key

Secret cryptographic information used to create authorised signatures under a particular system's rules. Someone obtaining a usable key may gain dangerous transaction authority. It is different from a public receiving address and should not be pasted into a message or this learning website. Wallet arrangements can require more than one key or approval. Related: signature, custody.

### G-RECOVERY-PHRASE — Recovery phrase

A secret sequence of words that can restore related keys in compatible wallet designs. It is not a universal recovery method for every wallet or service account. Protect it according to the specific verified arrangement; anyone obtaining a usable phrase may gain control. This website never needs your actual phrase. Aliases: seed phrase; mnemonic phrase. Related: wallet, private key.

### G-SIGNATURE — Signature

Cryptographic evidence created with a signing key for specified data or an action. What a signature authorises depends on the request and system; some requests have consequences beyond sign-in. Inspect the actual authority rather than relying on a friendly label. A signature does not independently prove someone's real-world identity or a promised later payment. Related: spending approval.

### G-TRANSACTION — Transaction

A request or recorded action processed under a crypto network's rules. Its status may be pending, successful or failed, depending on the system and action. Being included in a record does not always mean the intended action succeeded. Check the relevant result, asset movement and conditions rather than treating every visible transaction as payment receipt. Related: transaction identifier, confirmation.

### G-TRANSACTION-ID — Transaction identifier

An identifier used to locate a transaction record on a particular network, often called a transaction hash. It is a starting point for verification, not proof of a successful payment by itself. Use the correct network and inspect the actual status, destination and relevant asset movement. A screenshot containing an identifier still needs independent checking. Related: explorer.

### G-GAS — Gas

A measure of computational work used by Ethereum and some related systems to price execution. Gas units and the price paid for those units are different quantities. The action, network rules and current conditions affect the fee. Other networks can use different fee models. Do not treat an estimated fee as a guarantee of completion or receipt. Related: network fee.

### G-NETWORK-FEE — Network fee

A charge associated with processing activity under a network's rules. It differs from provider, conversion or payout charges. The fee asset may be different from the asset being transferred. Some included failed executions can still incur a fee. Confirm the applicable model and quoted conditions rather than assuming rejection, failure and successful transfer have identical costs. Related: gas, spread.

### G-STABLECOIN — Stablecoin

A crypto asset designed to track a reference value, such as a currency. Mechanisms and conditions differ. The target does not guarantee a market price, direct redemption eligibility or an amount received through local conversion. Identify the particular design, issuer or collateral and route before relying on a stability claim. Related: peg/reference value, issuer redemption.

### G-PEG — Peg / reference value

The value a pegged asset aims to track. A token targeting USD 1 does not establish that every market or conversion quote equals USD 1. The mechanism may be stressed or fail to maintain the target. Keep the intended reference, actual offered price and final proceeds separate when evaluating a claim. Related: stablecoin, conversion.

### G-ISSUER-REDEMPTION — Issuer redemption

Exchanging an eligible asset directly through its issuer under applicable terms. Holding a token does not automatically qualify someone for that route; account, jurisdiction and other conditions can apply. Selling through another service is a different arrangement. Check the particular issuer's current terms rather than generalising one token's redemption rules to all stablecoins. Related: conversion.

### G-CONVERSION — Conversion

Exchanging one asset or currency for another through a specified route. The quoted rate, spread, charges, availability and timing affect the result. A displayed crypto balance is different from completed conversion and from local-money payout. Use supplied fictional inputs for practice; this glossary supplies no live rate, available provider or permitted country route. Related: spread, issuer redemption.

### G-SPREAD — Spread

The difference between quoted buying and selling prices, or another specifically stated price comparison. A route may incorporate costs through its quote rather than a separately labelled fee. Ask which prices are being compared and what the actual proceeds would be. A low visible service fee does not by itself make a conversion route cheaper. Related: conversion, network fee.

### G-TOKEN-REPRESENTATION — Token representation

The particular form in which an asset is represented within a system, such as an issuer-native token deployment or a third-party wrapped version. Similar names and prices do not establish equivalent rights or receiving support. Identify the exact representation and network, then check current destination instructions. Related: blockchain network, stablecoin, receiving address.

### G-MEMO-TAG — Memo / tag

An additional destination detail some receiving systems require to identify the intended account or purpose. It can be necessary alongside an address. Check whether the particular route requires it, and use the exact supplied detail. Do not assume every network or provider uses one, or that address-format validation proves the full receiving instructions are satisfied. Related: receiving address.

### G-CONFIRMATION — Confirmation

Evidence of a transaction's inclusion and subsequent network progression as defined by the particular system. How confirmations are described and counted varies. A provider may state its own crediting requirement and processing step. Meeting a confirmation count does not independently prove provider credit or a local payout. Related: finality, transaction, explorer.

### G-FINALITY — Finality

The degree or condition under which a network treats an outcome as settled against reversal under its rules. Some systems use explicit finality conditions; others provide increasing confidence as the record progresses. Finality is network-specific and does not establish provider credit, conversion or bank/mobile-money receipt. Read the applicable model rather than applying one universal count. Related: confirmation.

### G-SPENDING-APPROVAL — Spending approval

Permission, in systems supporting it, for a specified address or contract to spend certain assets according to defined conditions. It is different from checking a balance or making one direct transfer. Inspect who receives authority, which asset and allowance are covered, and what revocation can change. A familiar logo or small initial request does not explain the full permission. Related: signature.

### G-EXPLORER — Explorer

A tool for viewing a network's records. Establish which network it shows and reach a trustworthy source independently. Inspect relevant status, destination and asset movement; search results and coloured ticks can require interpretation. An explorer record does not by itself verify a provider's account credit or a separate payout. This glossary does not select or endorse an explorer service. Related: transaction identifier.

### G-PHISHING — Phishing

A deceptive request designed to obtain secrets, access or consequential actions by appearing trustworthy. It can arrive through messages, fake support, websites or other channels. Verify independently and inspect what is requested. Never give this website your signing secrets. Recognising missing evidence is enough to pause; you do not have to prove the sender's intent first. Related: signature, recovery phrase.
